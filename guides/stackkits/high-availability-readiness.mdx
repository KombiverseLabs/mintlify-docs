High availability is an operating model, not a checkbox. Before you enable the [High Availability add-on](/stackkits/addons/high-availability), confirm that the basics are in place.

This guide is for the moment when "more reliable" starts to mean real customer, team, or business risk. It helps separate useful resilience planning from expensive overbuild.

## What this guide helps you decide

- whether downtime has real consequences
- whether you have enough nodes for the HA tier you are considering
- whether backups, monitoring, and incident ownership are ready
- whether a single-environment kit (Basement or Cloud) should still be the deployable first step

## Current status

High availability ships as the `ha` add-on — an overlay you enable on top of a StackKit deployment, not a separate kit. It comes in two tiers:

- **Warm standby** (≥ 2 nodes): continuous backup plus automated restore to a standby node; downtime equals restore time. Builds on the backup add-on.
- **Quorum** (≥ 3 odd manager nodes): etcd-quorum live failover with near-zero downtime.

The node floors are technical prerequisites (failover targets and quorum math), not pricing tiers. See the [add-on page](/stackkits/addons/high-availability) for how each tier works.

## Readiness checklist

You are closer to HA readiness when you can answer yes to most of these:

- There are enough suitable nodes for your target tier: at least two for warm standby, at least three (odd count) for quorum.
- Important services have documented recovery steps.
- Backups are tested, not only configured. (Warm standby *is* an automated restore — untested backups are the whole risk.)
- Monitoring exists outside the service being monitored.
- DNS and routing failure modes are understood.
- Someone owns incident response.
- You know which services actually require high availability.

## Common false signals

These do not automatically mean you need HA:

- owning multiple servers
- wanting faster services
- running public apps
- having many Docker containers
- wanting backups

Those needs may still fit a Basement Kit or Cloud Kit with add-ons like backup and monitoring, or a Modern Homelab for hybrid setups.

The stronger signal is not infrastructure size. It is the consequence of failure.

## When HA is justified

High availability is worth planning when downtime creates business, customer, or operational risk.

Examples:

- startup services with customer impact
- authentication or access services required by a team
- production apps with external users
- critical monitoring or routing infrastructure

If no one owns response and recovery, the setup is not highly available yet. It is only distributed.

## Which tier fits

- **Warm standby** fits when a short, bounded outage (the restore time) is acceptable and you have two nodes. It is the lower-cost entry point and doubles as continuous proof that your backups restore.
- **Quorum** fits when downtime is effectively unacceptable and you can dedicate at least three manager nodes (odd count). Live failover means no restore step on failure.

## How this maps to the finder

The finder treats startup intent, `3+` servers, public services, and startup-service use cases as HA signals. It may still recommend a single-environment kit (Basement or Cloud) as the deployable starting point while marking the High Availability add-on as the target direction.
